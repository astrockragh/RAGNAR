SEDM Sky-Line Calibration

SEDM is being reinstalled at LMU's 2.1 m telescope on the Wendelstein. Can it calibrate (a) its wavelength scale to 1 nm and (b) its relative spectral response from band fluxes measured on the night sky? We simulated the sky at R = λ/FWHM = 75, 100 and 150 over 340–1000 nm, and tested band-flux measurements on mock observations per resolution.

Units: R is resolving power. Line brightness is in rayleigh (R) and spectra are in R nm⁻¹. PWV and zenith distances are for the Wendelstein.

Short answers

(a) Wavelength calibration to 1 nm

Yes, 557–890 nm8-4 band marginalNothing below 557 nm

Eight sky features from [O I] 557.7 nm to the OH 7-3 band hold their positions to σ ≤ 0.5 nm (most ≤ 0.2 nm). The 8-4 blend at 939 nm scatters by σ = 1.2 nm. A 10% error in the assumed resolution moves the OH blends by 0.2–0.7 nm. In 340–550 nm the brightest airglow feature reaches only R nm⁻¹, against about 10 R nm⁻¹ of dark-sky continuum.

(b) Relative flux calibration

8-3/8-4: ±2%7-2/7-3: ±7%Needs PWV and a telluric curve

Two same-level band pairs carry the colour. With band fluxes alone, 8-3/8-4 gives the response ratio between 729 and 941 nm to ±2% (68%), and 7-2/7-3 the ratio between 686 and 884 nm to ±7%. A smooth response through both comes back to ±1.5–7% over 650–990 nm with about 100 sky spaxels. 8-4 sits in the 940 nm water band, so PWV must be known to 1–2 mm. The O₂ B band takes a fifth of the 7-2 flux, but it depends only on airmass. Once the background under 7-2 follows the telluric curve, the absorption no longer limits the pair.

(a) Wavelength calibration

Peak positions were measured as in the RAGNAR line lists, with a matched filter of the LSF width, over 1,080 simulated Wendelstein skies at R = 100. σλ is the scatter of each peak. The full range spans the extremes of every parameter at once. The last column gives the shift if the true resolution were 75 or 150.

  • The scatter meets the 1 nm budget everywhere except the 8-4 band. The 8-4 blend shape changes with Trot, moving its peak by up to 4 nm; use it with a low weight, or fit the sky model rather than the peak. The weak 6-1 blend and the 7-3 blend exceed 1 nm only at the extreme corners of the grid.
  • The resolution has to be known to roughly 10%. A wrong R changes the blend shapes. Fitting a model sky convolved with the LSF measured from arcs removes the problem.
  • Use the reference list made with the same peak finder. Raw peaks and matched-filter peaks of the same blend differ by up to 1.3 nm.
  • Below 557 nm the arcs must carry the solution. Light-pollution lines from the Munich area (not modelled) may help in the blue.

(b) Relative flux calibration

All flux measurements use the spectra without the centroider. The calibration target is the spectral shape of the response, normalised at 700 nm.

The groups that vary together

All lines from one upper vibrational level (v′) share that level's population, which carries all the night-to-night variation in vibrational excitation and chemistry. Between bands of the same level, only the rotational distribution matters: Trot and the hot population. Their ratios are set by the Einstein-A branching. Levels v′ = 6–9 each have two or three bands between 610 and 995 nm, and those carry the colour information.

PWV hits one band combination much harder than the others

The 8-4 band sits in the 940 nm water band, so the 8-3/8-4 ratio changes by about 1.5% per mm at low PWV, flattening as the strongest water lines saturate. The other same-level pairs change by 0.1–0.2% per mm. The 8-4 sky lines are also absorbed differently from a star's continuum: they fall partly on and partly between water lines. A response derived from 8-4 therefore needs a line-by-line telluric model, and cannot simply reuse a standard star's telluric correction.

How much the O₂ B band costs the 7-2/7-3 pair

  • Depth. The B band removes of the 7-2 band's flux, against for 7-3. Relative to zenith, the 7-2/7-3 ratio drops by at ZD 31° and 48°. PWV changes 7-2 by less than 0.5%.
  • It depends only on airmass. O₂ is well mixed, and the 7-2 transmission scales as airmass. A 1% error in pressure × airmass therefore costs 0.15%. In the mocks, knowing only the zenith distance worked as well as knowing the full atmosphere.
  • Predicting it line by line is fragile. The 7-2 lines sit on the wings of narrow O₂ lines. Shifting the OH line positions by 1 pm (0.001 nm) changes the predicted 7-2 transmission by about 2%, and by 5 pm up to 8%. The same shifts change 7-3 by under 0.05% and 8-4 by under 0.6%. Calibrating the 7-2/7-3 ratio against airmass once, empirically, is safer than trusting the prediction.
  • The continuum dips too. Under the 7-2 blend the lines see and the continuum . The B-band dip in the sky continuum is as large as the 7-2 emission. A linear background under 7-2 therefore biases its flux by about −10% (±15%). A background shaped like the telluric curve removes this.
  • The larger problem is O₂ emission. The O₂ b(0-1) band at 865 nm sits inside the 7-3 window and varies independently of OH. Scaling it by 0.5× or 1.5× moves the 7-3 flux by +27% or −24%, unless it is fitted as a nuisance term; with that term, by ±3%. The remaining ±7% scatter in 7-2/7-3 follows Trot (correlation 0.7) and the O₂/OH ratio, not airmass.

Mock observations: the response from band fluxes

Each mock is one random night: Trot 175–215 K, Tvib 7,500–12,500 K, hot population ×0.7–1.4, random chemistry for every species, PWV 0–27 mm, ZD 0–48°, continuum 5–25 R nm⁻¹ with a random slope, and an LSF width off by 3% (1σ) from the assumed one. Each is seen through a random smooth instrument response, sampled at 3 pixels per FWHM, with photon noise. Each band's flux comes from Gaussians at fixed positions and widths plus a background, over ±2 FWHM. The background is either linear, or linear times the telluric transmission curve at SEDM resolution. The O₂ b(0-1) blend is fitted as a nuisance term next to 7-3. The ratio of two band fluxes is divided by the ratio predicted for fiducial airglow through the assumed atmosphere. No model of the whole sky is fitted.

Noise levels are detected photons per rayleigh of line flux. A rough estimate is 5 per spaxel in 30 minutes, if the IFU keeps the étendue per spaxel it had on the P60 and the total throughput is about 20%. 5,000 is then most of the ~1,000 sky spaxels of one exposure, 500 is about 100 spaxels, and 50 about 10.

  • 8-3/8-4 is the workhorse. It comes back to ±1.6–2.1% at all three resolutions once the telluric curve is known, with a bias under 1%. Without the PWV it is off by +5% ± 8%. In an earlier run, PWV known to ±1.5 mm added about 1.5% in quadrature.
  • 7-2/7-3 reaches ±7% (±5% at R = 75). It is limited by Trot and O₂ emission, not by O₂ absorption.
  • The other pairs don't work as band fluxes. 6-1/6-2 scatters by ±12–25%, because 6-1 is weak and blended. 9-4/9-5 scatters by ±20–50%, because 9-5 is half 3-0.
  • A response through both pairs comes back to ±1.5–3.5% at 686, 730, 940 and 990 nm, and to ±5–7% in between. Each pair fixes only a ratio, so the curve between the pairs depends on the assumed smooth shape.
  • A full fit of the sky would do better. It reached 0.2–1% in our mocks, but it needs a sky model good enough for every band, which we do not assume here.

What the mocks do not include

  • Errors in the Einstein-A branching ratios. The mocks use the same A values for the truth and the predicted ratios. An error in the 8-3/8-4 or 7-2/7-3 band ratio enters the response directly. Noll et al. (2020) found systematic errors in all six published A sets they tested. Measuring the two pair ratios once against a spectrophotometric standard fixes them. After that, the sky alone tracks the response.
  • A real OH population has more freedom than the model's cold-plus-hot distribution. This matters most for 7-2/7-3, which is already the most sensitive to Trot.
  • The O₂ b(0-1) nuisance term is a single Gaussian. The real band has two branches, and its shape changes slightly with temperature.
  • The Wendelstein atmosphere is approximated. We used Mauna Kea MODTRAN curves with the slant O₂, Rayleigh and H₂O columns matched. Ozone, aerosols and the effective extinction of a diffuse source are not modelled.

For reference: peaks from a single upper state need R ≳ 700

The original question asked for peaks that are ≥95–99% from one upper state (v′, J′, F). At R = 75–150 no OH peak gets more than about 45% from one upper state. Pairs at ≥95% purity, ≥100 nm apart and ≥5 R bright first appear at R ≈ 700, and at ≥99% at R ≈ 1500. 8-4 P1(3.5) never reaches 99%, because 8-4 Q1(6.5) and R2(11.5) lie within 0.035 nm of it.

What the simulation leaves out

  • Blue airglow. The O₂ Herzberg and Chamberlain bands are not in RAGNAR. They add faint, broad structure below about 450 nm, not clean features.
  • Light pollution and moonlight. Lamp lines could help the blue wavelength scale. Moonlight raises the continuum and adds solar absorption features; the forward model would need a solar template term.
  • Continuum shape. The mocks use a smooth power-law continuum. Real airglow continuum (NO₂, FeO) has broad structure near 600 nm, below the fitted range.

Method and files

RAGNAR (fast version), resolution mode, 334–1015 nm, Gaussian LSF at λ/FWHM = 75, 100, 150. RAGNAR grid: Trot 180/210 K, Tvib 8000/12000 K, hot population ×0.75/1/1.33, five chemistry draws, three LSF widths, Wendelstein PWV 2.7/10.6/20 mm and ZD 0/48° (Mauna Kea MODTRAN at PWV 2/8/15 mm, ZD 40/60°). Mocks use a vectorised copy of RAGNAR's line model, checked against it to 10⁻¹³, with the full Mauna Kea PWV 0–20 mm × ZD 40/50/60° grid. The transmission panel is MODTRAN smoothed to the selected resolution; the sky-line transmission is weighted by the line flux at each wavelength. Line lists: lineLists/sedm_R*_{0,1}_wst_20261116. Scripts and tables: RAGNAR_new/sedm/ (run_sedm.py, analyze_sedm.py, mock_sedm.py).
